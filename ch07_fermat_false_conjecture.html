Fermat's False Guess — Killed by One Number
Fermat conjectured: every number of the form 2^(2ⁿ)+1 is prime. It held for n=0,1,2,3,4. Click to test the next n on the visual timeline — watch where it breaks.
The aha: this actually happened — Fermat's conjecture held for 5 confirmed cases before Euler found n=5 breaks it in 1732, nearly a century later. One counterexample, after ANY number of successes, ends a "for all" claim instantly and permanently.
How did Euler actually FIND 641? Not by brute-force guessing.
Testing whether a 10-digit number like 4,294,967,297 is prime by trying every possible divisor would have taken forever by hand. Euler didn't guess — he proved a structural fact first: any prime factor of Fₙ (for n≥2) must have the specific form k·2ⁿ⁺¹ + 1. For n=5, that means only numbers of the form 64k+1 are even candidates. This shrank an impossibly large search down to a short, checkable list:

This is the same lesson Chapter 7 keeps returning to: a "for all" claim is fragile (one failure ends it), but proving it false efficiently still takes real mathematical structure, not luck. Euler's insight about the 64k+1 form is itself a small "for all" theorem — a general fact about every possible factor, discovered by reasoning, that then made the specific counterexample findable at all.
